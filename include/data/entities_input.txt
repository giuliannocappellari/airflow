The moon’s light sifted through the vaulted windows of the old observatory, silvering every mote of dust that drifted in the hush between words. Professor Aurelia Crane paced beside the brass-rimmed telescope, her long coat whispering against the cracked mosaic floor.
“Marcus,” she said at last, “if we don’t re-align the lenses tonight, tomorrow’s transit will be lost for another century.”
Marcus Vale—tall, always a little rumpled—ran a hand through the curls that never stayed combed. “Professor, the primary mirror is shattered. You can’t stitch starlight together with wishful thinking.”
From a shadow near the instrument racks, Eve Callahan emerged, carrying a case of delicate glass discs. She set them on a workbench with the quiet assurance of someone who’d spent a childhood repairing clockwork toys in secret. “We won’t need the original mirror,” she said, flicking back an errant braid. “I’ve ground four substitutes. They’re smaller, but arranged correctly they’ll give us a composite aperture.”
Aurelia’s brows arched. “An interferometer?”
“Close enough,” Eve murmured. “Light behaves if you ask politely.”
At the doorway stood Lieutenant León Ortega, uniform dusty from the climb, lantern in hand. “The city patrol is sweeping the hill. They think we’re rousing rebels up here. We’ve got maybe an hour before they cordon the grounds.”
“Then we have fifty-nine minutes to make history,” Aurelia declared. She spun to Marcus. “Fasten the auxiliary mounts. Every second matters.”
Marcus hesitated only a heartbeat, then set to work, brass gears clicking under his deft fingers. Eve slid the first glass disc into its cradle; moonlight fractured into iridescent fans across the walls. León extinguished the lantern to preserve their dark-adapted eyes, but stayed by the door, hand hovering near the holster no one acknowledged. Outside, distant sirens drifted like wounded gulls over cobbled streets.
As the four labored—the scholar, the skeptic, the artisan, and the sentinel—the dome around them seemed to inhale, drawing celestial breath. Star-sharp silence reigned until the improvised array found its focus. Aurelia leaned over the eyepiece, and her gasp was almost a prayer.
“There,” she whispered, voice trembling. “A ring of flame around a night-black pearl. The eclipse of Galene’s sixth moon. Recorded, at last.”
Somewhere below, boots pounded stone steps, growing louder. But in that fragile, luminous instant, the universe itself felt close enough to touch, and the four companions knew—whatever fate broke upon the observatory doors—they had caged a miracle in glass and memory.
